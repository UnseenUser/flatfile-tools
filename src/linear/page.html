Saw and stock

Measure the kerf from a cut you made rather than reading the blade. On twenty cuts a half-millimetre error is a centimetre of bar, which is exactly how the last piece ends up short. Everything here is unitless: work in millimetres, inches or feet, as long as you use the same one everywhere.

What this does that a formula cannot

Working out how much bar a job needs is arithmetic. Working out which pieces share which bar is not: it is bin packing, and the number of ways to arrange thirty pieces across ten bars is larger than anything you would check by hand. This runs a real packing pass over your list, tries two dozen arrangements and keeps the best one it finds.

It does not promise the mathematical optimum, because nothing that returns an answer in a fraction of a second can. It promises a plan that is normally within one bar of the best possible, and it shows you the yield so you can judge it yourself.

Why your yield is sometimes bad and nothing can fix it

If a piece is longer than half a bar, no second piece can ever join it. Ten pieces at 3.1m out of 6m bars is ten bars at 52% yield, and that is not the plan being poor, it is the job being shaped that way. The only thing that changes it is a different stock length. When this happens the tool says so under the results, rather than leaving you to wonder.

The kerf, and the direction the error runs

Every cut turns a few millimetres into dust. Each piece here costs its own length plus one kerf, including the last piece on the bar, because cutting it free of the remainder is a real cut. When pieces happen to finish flush with the end, that charges one kerf more than reality. That is deliberate. A plan that is one kerf pessimistic leaves a few millimetres spare; a plan that is one kerf optimistic leaves the last piece short, and a short piece is scrap.

Offcuts are not waste

Set offcut worth keeping to the shortest remainder you would actually rack rather than skip, and the results mark which bars leave something worth putting back. On a repeating job that figure is often the difference between the material cost you planned and the one you paid.

It sends nothing anywhere

This page runs entirely in your browser. There is no account, no upload and no server: once the page has loaded it contacts nothing at all, and it keeps working with the network off. Nothing you type is sent anywhere, which for anyone pricing a job from a client's drawings is the only arrangement worth having.
