Divisions decide how round the round end really is. Six is the usual minimum, twelve for anything that has to look round, and more than that stops mattering before it stops costing you marking time.

How a pattern is actually developed

You cannot flatten a cone by measuring the drawing, because every sloping line you see in plan is foreshortened. The real length of a sloping edge is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose base is the distance between its ends in plan and whose height is the rise. Get that one idea and the rest is bookkeeping.

A square to round is then built as four flat triangles, one standing on each side of the rectangle, with a cone quarter at each corner. The corner is approximated by a fan of triangles running from the rectangle corner out to points around the circle, which is exactly what you would do by hand, with more patience than most people have.

The numbers matter more than the picture

The drawing on this page shows you the shape and where the folds go. What you take to the plate is the table of true lengths: swing an arc from each rectangle corner at each listed radius, step round, and join. That is how these have been marked out since long before anyone had a printer that could manage a 1200mm sheet, and it works at any size.

What this does not do

No bend allowance and no thickness. The pattern is the developed surface, so for anything but thin sheet you need to work to the neutral axis yourself. No seam or lap allowance either: add what your joint needs. And it assumes the two ends are parallel.
