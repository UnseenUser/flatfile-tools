What a cut list actually has to get right

A cutting layout is not a packing puzzle. It is a packing puzzle with three constraints that most online calculators quietly drop, and dropping any one of them produces a plan that looks efficient on screen and does not work at the saw.

The blade takes a few millimetres every time

Every cut turns some of the board into dust. That width is the kerf, and it is usually around 3mm on a table saw and 2.4mm on a track saw, but the only number worth using is the one you measure from a cut you made yourself. Half a millimetre of error does not matter once. Across twelve cuts on one sheet it is why the last part comes out 6mm short. This calculator subtracts the kerf from the material at every cut rather than treating cuts as free.

A panel saw cuts all the way across

Layouts that nest parts into pockets assume you can stop a cut in the middle of a sheet. On a table saw or a panel saw you cannot. Each cut runs edge to edge and splits the piece in two, and every later cut happens on one of those halves. These are guillotine cuts, and a layout that respects them is slightly less efficient on paper and is the only kind you can actually follow. Everything here is guillotine cuts.

Grain runs one way

Rotating a part ninety degrees often saves material, and on anything with a visible face it ruins the piece. Lock the grain on parts that will be seen and leave it unlocked on the rest, and the layout takes the saving where it is free.

What to do with the plan

Cut the cheapest sheet first. Check the first two parts against the plan with a tape before you cut the third. If they are out, the kerf you entered is wrong, and you have lost one sheet instead of four.
